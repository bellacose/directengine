A first laser for selling work should match what you plan to sell. The laser type decides which materials you can use, the work area decides how big your pieces can be, and the safety class and ventilation decide where the machine can go. This guide walks through those choices with example machines and the specs their makers publish. Prices were read from the makers' own sites on November 15, 2026; several were sale prices, and they change often.

## Start with what you'll make

Write down the three products you most expect to sell, with their material and size. Then check:

- Can this laser type handle that material? (See below and our guide to laser materials.)
- Does the work area fit the largest piece?
- Do you need to cut through the material, or only engrave or mark its surface?

## Laser types

| Type | What it does | Example limits from makers |
|---|---|---|
| Diode | Blue-light lasers. Glowforge's run at 450 nm. | Glowforge's 6-watt, 450 nm diode machines take materials up to 1/4 inch thick, and its Spark can't do glass, clear, translucent or white acrylic, or some blue acrylics ([Glowforge](https://glowforge.com/b/tech-specs/craft)). |
| CO2 | Epilog lists wood, acrylic, leather, paper, fabric, glass, ceramic, marble and tile among materials compatible with its CO2 lasers ([Epilog](https://www.epiloglaser.com/how-it-works/laser-material-compatibility/)). | Mark bare metal only with a metal marking solution ([Epilog](https://www.epiloglaser.com/how-it-works/laser-material-compatibility/)). |
| Fiber | OMTech's fiber marker runs at 1064 nm and is sold for "etching precious and base metals" ([OMTech](https://omtechlaser.com/products/50w-fmm-5rw2-us)). | OMTech lists a maximum engraving depth of 0.1 mm per pass for its 50 W fiber marker ([OMTech](https://omtechlaser.com/products/50w-fmm-5rw2-us)). Epilog's fiber material list covers marking metals and plastics ([Epilog](https://www.epiloglaser.com/how-it-works/laser-material-compatibility/)). |

Materials that are unsafe to laser are unsafe on every type. PVC, for one, produces hydrogen chloride gas ([MIT EHS](https://ehs.mit.edu/workplace-safety-program/laser-cutter-safety/)).

## Safety class and enclosure

The FDA classes lasers by hazard. Class 1 is "considered non-hazardous". Class 4 is an "immediate skin hazard and eye hazard from exposure to either the direct or reflected beam" and "may also present a fire hazard" ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/radiation-emitting-products/home-business-and-entertainment-products/laser-products-and-instruments)).

A machine can contain a Class 4 laser and still be rated Class 1 as a whole, because the enclosure keeps the beam in: Glowforge describes Aura as "a Class 1 laser product containing an embedded Class 4 laser" ([Glowforge](https://glowforge.com/b/tech-specs/craft)). An open machine, like OMTech's fiber marker, ships with a pair of protective eyewear ([OMTech](https://omtechlaser.com/products/50w-fmm-5rw2-us)); with an open machine the beam isn't contained, so follow the maker's eye protection instructions for everyone in the room.

Never defeat a laser's interlocks; MIT notes that this "could allow the beam to escape" ([MIT EHS](https://ehs.mit.edu/workplace-safety-program/laser-cutter-safety/)).

## Ventilation and fire

- **Fumes have to go somewhere.** Glowforge requires either its own filter or an open-able window within 8 feet ([Glowforge Craft](https://glowforge.com/b/tech-specs/craft), [Glowforge Performance](https://glowforge.com/b/tech-specs/performance)). UW–Madison says laser cutters "require proper ventilation to prevent fires and exposure hazards" ([UW–Madison EHS](https://ehs.wisc.edu/laser-cutter-safety/)).
- **Stay with it.** "Never leave an operating laser cutter unattended" ([MIT EHS](https://ehs.mit.edu/workplace-safety-program/laser-cutter-safety/)).
- **Extinguisher.** MIT says one should be mounted on the wall near the cutter ([MIT EHS](https://ehs.mit.edu/workplace-safety-program/laser-cutter-safety/)).
- **Air assist.** MIT calls it "important for preventing fires (and also helps to make cleaner cuts)" ([MIT EHS](https://ehs.mit.edu/workplace-safety-program/laser-cutter-safety/)).

Plan the spot before you buy: near a window or an exhaust route, away from anything that burns easily, with room to stand and watch.

## Software

Some machines come with their own software: OMTech's fiber marker, for example, ships with EzCad2 ([OMTech](https://omtechlaser.com/products/50w-fmm-5rw2-us)). Many others use LightBurn, which sells two licenses:

- **LightBurn Core, $99:** for GCode-based controllers (GRBL, Smoothieware, Marlin, GRBL-LPC, Cohesion3D) ([LightBurn](https://lightburnsoftware.com/products/lightburn-core)).
- **LightBurn Pro, $199:** adds DSP controllers (Ruida, Trocen, TopWisdom) and galvo lasers (EZCad2, EZCad3, BSL; fiber, CO2 or UV) ([LightBurn](https://lightburnsoftware.com/products/lightburn-pro)).

Both licenses allow activation on up to three computers, include a year of updates, and keep working after the update year ends; LightBurn has a free trial so you can test your hardware first ([LightBurn Core](https://lightburnsoftware.com/products/lightburn-core), [LightBurn Pro](https://lightburnsoftware.com/products/lightburn-pro)). Check which controller a machine uses before you buy, so you know which license, if any, you'll need. OMTech includes a LightBurn license with the Polar Lite ([OMTech](https://omtech.com/products/omtech-polar-lite-55w-desktop-co2-laser-engraver-and-cutter)).

## When to use a local shop instead

Buying isn't the only way to start. Paying a local laser shop can make sense when:

- you want to test whether a product sells before buying a machine;
- the job needs a laser type you don't have, such as metal marking when you own a CO2 or diode laser;
- the piece is bigger than your work area;
- you have no safe place to vent fumes.
